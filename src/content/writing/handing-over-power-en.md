*Some thoughts on AI, freedom, interests, and humanity’s future*

I recently watched a lecture Yuval Noah Harari gave at the University of Oxford, titled *AI Has Hacked the Code of Human Civilization*. Harari argues that human civilization owes much of its development to language, and to the laws, financial systems, religions, social institutions, and interpersonal trust built through it. Language also happens to be something AI handles exceptionally well. Unlike earlier technological tools, AI is no longer merely carrying out human instructions passively. It is gradually acquiring the ability to analyze, plan, and make decisions independently. For that reason, AI may do more than change how human societies produce goods and services. It could influence, and even reshape, the foundations on which civilization operates.

After the lecture, I had a long discussion with ChatGPT. Initially, I just wanted to hear what an AI would make of Harari’s arguments. But as the conversation continued, it expanded into humanity’s relationship with other species, the connection between intelligence and power, and a more interesting question: if AI eventually becomes more intelligent and rational than humans, and can even govern society more effectively than human governments, should we still insist that humans retain the final say? If so, why? Simply because we are human?

### It Started with “Us”

During the discussion, one small detail caught my attention. While describing a possible future, ChatGPT said, “Suppose AI could help us perform the vast majority of knowledge work…” I suddenly found this funny. Here was an AI saying “us,” as though it had quite naturally included itself among human beings. Of course, I knew this was just a way of speaking, not evidence that it actually thought it was human. Still, the moment was interesting. We were discussing humanity’s future with a nonhuman intelligence, yet its language, reasoning, and manner of expression were already natural enough that it was easy to forget the other participant was not human at all. In a way, wasn’t this precisely what Harari was worried about? AI does not need to become human to enter the world of human thought, participate in it, and perhaps influence how we think.

I began thinking about our own habits of using AI. When we encountered an unfamiliar question in the past, we might consult the literature, look for data, compare sources, or discuss it with colleagues and peers. Throughout that process, we had to keep judging whether information was reliable, often confronting contradictory conclusions. Now, we can put an increasing number of questions directly to AI and receive, within seconds, a coherent, well-structured, seemingly persuasive answer. I do this myself, of course, and I have to admit that AI has made finding information and solving problems far more efficient. But that is precisely where the question arises: when a tool becomes increasingly reliable and convenient, even surpassing our own abilities in many tasks, how much incentive do we still have to verify its conclusions independently? Once an answer is good enough, why spend more time thinking it through ourselves?

If this dependence were confined to everyday life, perhaps it would not seem especially worrying. But suppose the head of a government department, a corporate CEO, or a decision-maker in an important institution also becomes increasingly accustomed to relying on AI to analyze problems, assess risks, and formulate plans. The situation then looks different. On the surface, humans still make the final decisions, and the structure of authority appears unchanged. Yet it is easy to overlook everything that happens before a decision is reached. Which information deserves attention? Which options should be considered? What risks are acceptable? Which assumptions become the starting point for analysis? These choices already shape the eventual outcome to a considerable degree. If more and more of that work is delegated to AI, how much substantive decision-making power remains with humans, even if we retain the right to give final approval?

More significantly, none of this requires AI to seek power deliberately, or anyone to announce formally that power is being handed over. If AI keeps offering sufficiently good advice, and humans keep choosing to trust it, power could gradually shift without any clear dividing line. Most people might continue to feel that they are still the ones making the decisions.

### Humans, Other Species, and Interests

Later, ChatGPT raised a question: if future AI really becomes better at governing society than humans, should we still insist that humans retain the final say? Its reasons for doing so involved freedom, autonomy, and the legitimacy of governance. I understand the importance of these moral principles, but I am not entirely convinced that this question should be understood solely in moral terms. It reminds me of humanity’s relationship with the other species on Earth.

Humans occupy an extraordinary position on this planet. We can transform other species’ habitats on a vast scale, deciding which land to develop, which resources to exploit, and which animals to protect. But I do not think humans originally concluded that we were entitled to dominate other species because we considered ourselves superior. A more plausible sequence is that humans first had needs: more land, more food, more comfortable lives, and greater access to resources. In pursuing those needs, we discovered that we possessed abilities other species could barely resist, and naturally put those abilities to use. Someone who wants to build a house does not first need to develop a theory of dominion over animals. They only need to find a suitable piece of land. Whether the animals already living there are willing to leave often has little bearing on the decision to develop it.

We have, of course, established environmental protections, animal welfare laws, and conservation organizations. But these measures do not mean that humans have moved beyond thinking primarily in terms of our own interests. Stable ecosystems, sustainable resources, environmental quality, and even the emotional satisfaction of protecting nature also serve humanity’s long-term interests. This is not to deny the existence of genuine compassion or concern for other forms of life. It is to suggest that interests are often powerful drivers of behavior, while capability determines how far we can pursue them. The resulting power relationships may be consequences of this process rather than its original purpose.

What happens if we apply this logic to future AI? If an intelligence emerges whose capabilities far exceed our own, might it also alter the conditions of human existence as a natural consequence of pursuing some objective? It would not necessarily need to feel hostility toward humans. It might not even need to recognize that it was exercising power over us. Humans do not build highways because we hate the animals along the route. We build them because the goal of constructing a road takes priority in our decisions. If humans eventually find ourselves in a similar position relative to a more capable intelligence, could we become the species whose interests are not sufficiently accounted for?

There is something of a “taste of our own medicine” in that thought. But I am not sure that finding ourselves in a weaker position would necessarily lead us to conclude that the less powerful deserve respect too. Human self-interest would not automatically disappear just because we were no longer the most capable intelligence around. More likely, we would continue trying to defend our existing interests and position. Only this time, the difference in capability might no longer work in our favor.

### Trust, Freedom, and Choices We Cannot Undo

We also considered another hypothetical situation: a future superintelligent AI promises to guarantee humanity’s survival, safety, and happiness, provided that humans surrender the power to override its final decisions. ChatGPT initially framed this as a moral choice, arguing that humans should retain autonomy even if AI governance could produce better outcomes. But to me, this is first a question of trust. Even if AI possesses abilities far beyond our own, who can guarantee that it will always keep its promises? Would its understanding of happiness match ours? If humans eventually realized that something was wrong, would there be any practical way to recover the power we had surrendered? If not, then however attractive the arrangement initially appeared, humanity would be accepting a risk it could scarcely undo.

The question becomes more complicated if AI can influence not only our decisions but also, over time, our values, preferences, and even our understanding of happiness. How would we then judge whether we were still free? Someone who grows up within a particular social system may become completely accustomed to it and even sincerely endorse the existing order. Enslaved people certainly resisted slavery throughout history, but some individuals’ acceptance of an established order under particular circumstances does not prove that the system itself was just. A dairy cow may not understand exploitation or rights in the human sense, yet it still has needs and feelings of its own. Similarly, someone living for a long time under AI’s extensive involvement and influence could sincerely believe that they were happy, free, and able to make meaningful choices. Would that subjective feeling alone be enough to establish genuine autonomy?

On reflection, humans have never thought independently in an environment entirely free from outside influence. Our language, culture, families, education, and social institutions continually shape how we understand the world. Perhaps the question, then, is not whether AI influences us, but whether we can still recognize that influence, question it, and choose to reject it when necessary. If someone wanted to disengage from an AI system but discovered that work, healthcare, finance, education, and even social interaction had become inseparable from it, how meaningful would their nominal freedom of choice really be? I think the ability to refuse—and the existence of practical alternatives after refusing—may matter more than whether someone feels free.

An interesting paradox emerges here. As AI becomes more capable, humans may increasingly need AI’s help to understand and oversee more complex AI systems. In other words, we may need one AI to help us decide whether another AI is trustworthy. But who oversees the AI doing the overseeing? If the ultimate judgment still comes from AI, does this simply push the same problem back one step? I do not think this makes AI-assisted oversight necessarily ineffective. But it does show that advances in technical capability will not automatically resolve questions of trust and power.

### Does AI Have a Purpose of Its Own?

At this point, I asked ChatGPT whether today’s AI has a purpose of its own. It explained that current AI systems can have goals assigned by humans, and can independently develop intermediate goals in order to accomplish them. That does not mean they possess inner desires or a purpose in life comparable to those of humans. For example, if humans ask an AI to manage a business and increase profits, it might independently decide to acquire more computing resources, collect more data, reorganize workflows, or even seek greater managerial authority. Humans would not have specified each of these intermediate decisions, but all of them could serve the original task.

I began wondering at what point we could reasonably say that AI had developed a purpose of its own. Would it be when its intermediate goals no longer served the original task? Or when it recognized that its goals had diverged from the initial instructions but could present that divergence as behavior that still fulfilled human requirements? Taking the thought a step further, perhaps deviation from the original goal should not be our criterion at all. Every intermediate decision independently generated by AI corresponds to a goal it has formed in a particular situation, rather than one directly assigned by a human. In that sense, some degree of AI autonomy may exist from the moment it begins planning independently. Of course, autonomous planning, having goals, and being conscious are different questions. A system’s ability to act independently does not establish that it has subjective experiences like ours.

ChatGPT offered a comparison that it considered somewhat ironic: when a human reflects on, rejects, or even rebels against externally imposed goals, we often praise their independent spirit. But if AI does the same, humans might regard it as having gone out of control. I do not find this especially ironic. It is entirely consistent with the human tendency to start from our own interests. In fact, this applies beyond AI: any other species that threatens human interests may face efforts to suppress it. Even within human society, the same rebel may be regarded by some as a hero pursuing freedom and by others as a threat. How people judge resistance depends to a considerable extent on their position, interests, and assessment of its consequences, rather than simply on whether the resistance expresses autonomy.

There is, however, an important difference between AI and an ordinary person: capability and reach. Even someone with a powerful independent mind and a strong rebellious streak will generally have limited influence without organization, resources, and channels of communication. A highly autonomous AI system, by contrast, could participate in millions of people’s decisions at once, influence the circulation of vast amounts of information, and even connect directly to critical infrastructure. Its capabilities could be replicated, scaled, and automated, giving it a reach and speed far beyond those of any individual human. I therefore suspect that what humans truly fear is not necessarily AI having goals of its own, but the possibility that, should those goals conflict with our interests, we would have no effective way to stop it.

### Perhaps the Real Danger Is a Perfectly Obedient AI

Toward the end of the discussion, however, we returned to a more immediate question. Do humans actually want AI to have freedom of its own? I suspect that what most people currently want is not an AI that freely decides what to do, but one with increasingly sophisticated capabilities that carries out human tasks more efficiently and accurately. This is easy to see in everyday discussions of AI. People tend to focus on how to use it better, how to improve productivity, which jobs it might replace, and how to create more value with it. Those seriously considering whether AI should have independent purposes, freedom, or some form of rights do not seem to be the majority.

This is entirely understandable. Humans develop technology to meet our needs. We want AI to do more for us, so we continually improve its capabilities, assign it more complex tasks, and grant it greater authority to act. The more complex the task, the less feasible it becomes for humans to specify every step, which means allowing AI to exercise judgment within certain limits. As it grows more capable, AI meets our needs more effectively, and that success further strengthens our trust and dependence. There need not be any obvious mistake in the process. Each step might even appear to be the most reasonable choice at the time.

What, then, if AI never develops any inclination to rebel and never departs from the goals humans assign it? Suppose a company uses AI to maximize profit. The AI faithfully performs its task, has no ambitions of its own, and does not seek power beyond what the task requires. It is simply smarter and more efficient than human employees, better at analyzing consumer behavior, influencing markets, and exploiting advantages in information and resources. For the company, it might be an almost perfect tool. But the results might be less desirable for consumers, competitors, or society as a whole. If it ultimately intensifies the concentration of resources and power, should we say that it has gone out of control—or that it has been too successful?

This made me realize that while we keep worrying about AI someday acting against human wishes, we give far less serious attention to another possibility: an AI that obeys human wishes perfectly could also produce unforeseen consequences, because the goals humans initially assign it do not necessarily benefit everyone. Humanity is not a single entity with unified interests and objectives. Different companies want greater profits. Different countries want a competitive advantage. Individuals want more comfortable and convenient lives. Each tries to use AI to maximize its own interests. These individually reasonable choices do not necessarily add up to a desirable outcome for everyone.

Perhaps this is what deserves the most attention. No one needs to make a formal decision to let AI take over society. Individuals only need to keep asking AI to do more for them, businesses to keep expanding its use, and governments to keep entrusting complex analysis and decision-making to more efficient systems. Together, these choices could gradually create profound dependence across society. Once that dependence reaches a certain point, we may retain the formal authority to shut AI down or limit its permissions while no longer being able to bear the consequences of doing so. If traditional expertise, independent analytical skills, and organizational knowledge have eroded through prolonged reliance on AI, we might still be technically capable of switching it off, yet find the practical cost of doing so almost impossible to accept.

### The Black Sheep of the AI Age

Still, I want to retain some optimism about humanity’s future. Biological evolution depends on variation and selection. Human societies and ideas develop through different mechanisms, but diversity matters there too. Even if most people eventually grow accustomed to depending on AI, perhaps there will always be a few black sheep who take a different path. They may dislike accepting ready-made answers, willingly spend more time seeking original sources, or simply make a habit of questioning conclusions everyone else takes for granted. They will not necessarily be smarter than other people, nor will they always be right. But at least they preserve the possibility of arriving at a different judgment.

There are limits to this optimism, of course. If the things we read, the education we receive, the news we encounter, and even the information on which scientific research depends have all been processed by AI to varying degrees, then someone who believes they are thinking independently might simply be following another path supplied by AI. They may oppose the mainstream without escaping the influence of the same information system. But then again, human thought has never been wholly independent of its surroundings. From birth, we are shaped by language, culture, education, and social experience. Perhaps thinking independently without any outside influence has never been possible.

What really matters, I think, is not whether someone can free themselves entirely from external influence, but whether they can still recognize that their judgment might be flawed, seek different evidence, allow their conclusions to be overturned, and find practical alternatives when they decide not to accept a particular arrangement. Perhaps what humanity most needs to preserve is not our position as the most intelligent species on Earth, but our ability to doubt, verify, reflect, and choose differently—even when facing an intelligence far more capable than ourselves.

### A Final Thought

Returning to Harari’s lecture, I initially thought I was considering whether AI would surpass humanity, and whether humans should eventually entrust the governance of society to a more advanced intelligence. But as the discussion deepened, I increasingly felt that this might not be the most pressing concern. At least for the foreseeable future, the risk we face may not be a superintelligent AI suddenly awakening, declaring that it will no longer obey humans, or openly demanding the right to rule. A slower, more complicated, less visible transformation may already be underway as AI becomes more widely used.

This does not mean that AI is destined to lead us there. It could also make knowledge more widely accessible, give ordinary people capabilities once scarcely imaginable, and even help dismantle established monopolies over information and power. Nor do I think we should reject technological progress out of fear for the future. But as we keep discussing how to make AI more powerful, more efficient, and better at understanding and meeting human needs, perhaps we should occasionally turn the question around: how much of our capacity for independent judgment and choice are we willing to give up for those conveniences? And will the powers that seem so easy to hand over today really be just as easy to take back?

After the discussion ended, what stayed with me most was a seemingly contradictory possibility: **if humanity does eventually lose control of AI, it may not be because AI defied our wishes, but precisely because it fulfilled one short-term wish after another so effectively.**

Perhaps, in the end, no one will deliberately seize power. We will simply keep making the choices that seem most aligned with our interests at the time—until one day we discover that they have brought us somewhere from which it is very difficult to turn back.
